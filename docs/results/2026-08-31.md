# 2026-08-31: pgbench scale 100, three legs and what the store wrote

tamnd/zou#1 asks for pgbench at scale 100 on a local filesystem, on MinIO, and on real S3, published as a table.
This is that table for the first two legs.
Real S3 is not here because there are no credentials for a real bucket, so the line stays open and the object store column below is MinIO on the same box as the database, which is the cheapest possible object store and should be read as the optimistic end of that column rather than as a stand in for S3.

The three legs are vanilla Postgres 18 on local disk, zou on a filesystem store, and zou on MinIO, at scale 100, 8 clients, 60 s a measurement, with the database loaded fresh at the start of each write leg.
Every number is from one quiet ten minute window, 05:06 to 05:16 UTC, with nothing else running on the box.

## The box and the settings, said up front

gamingpc, a 13th generation Core i9-13900K, 32 GB, Ubuntu 26.04 inside WSL2 on kernel 6.18.33.2, all three stores and both data directories on the same ext4 filesystem.
All three legs saw 16 of the 32 threads, pinned with `taskset -c 8-23`, which is what the environment stamp in each result file records.
MinIO is our own instance on 127.0.0.1:9100 over `/home/zoubench/minio-data`, not the one the box owner runs on 9000.

Two configuration differences matter more than anything else on this page and neither of them is a design difference.

The vanilla leg is Ubuntu's PostgreSQL 18.6 package at stock settings, which is 128 MB of shared buffers with full page writes on.
The zou legs are the vendored 18.4 with the zou patches as `zou dev` configures it, which is 7.8 GB of shared buffers with full page writes off.
So vanilla got a sixtieth of the memory for the same 1.5 GB database, and it still won every row below.
The resident set column is therefore a report of what each leg was handed and not a claim about either one, and the vanilla numbers are the low end of what that binary can do on this box rather than a tuned baseline.

The second is that the two zou legs did not start from stores in the same state: the filesystem store held 55 MB when its write leg started and the MinIO store held 3.29 GB left over from earlier runs.
That is visible in the counters and it is one of the two candidates for the split described further down.

## The write mix

pgbench tpcb-like, 8 clients, 8 threads, 60 s, scale 100, database loaded fresh immediately before.

| zou-localfs | 763.46 | 7.062 | 22.879 | 36.812 | 97.738 | 417.8 | 16.0 | 9.73 GiB | 58 |
| zou-minio | 842.07 | 8.062 | 13.316 | 18.050 | 294.570 | 1388.3 | 19.7 | 16.21 GiB | 37 |

zou is at 49 per cent of vanilla on a filesystem store and 54 per cent on MinIO, and takes 1.5x and 1.9x as long to load the same data.
No transaction failed on any leg.

## The read only mix

pgbench select-only, 8 clients, 8 threads, 60 s, against the database the write leg left behind.

| leg | tps | p50 ms | p95 ms | p99 ms | p999 ms | max ms | rss peak | cpu s |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pg18 | 102006.76 | 0.068 | 0.115 | 0.135 | 0.438 | 214.6 | 1.46 GiB | 0 |
| zou-localfs | 36660.26 | 0.204 | 0.294 | 0.459 | 1.747 | 9.1 | 13.79 GiB | 4 |
| zou-minio | 34042.52 | 0.225 | 0.313 | 0.390 | 0.652 | 13.8 | 14.52 GiB | 16 |

36 and 33 per cent of vanilla, and the object store costs 7 per cent against the filesystem rather than anything larger, because at this scale the working set is in shared buffers on both and the store is barely asked.
The tail is the other way round from the write mix: zou's worst read is 9 to 14 ms and vanilla's is 215 ms.

## Inside the minute

The single tps number hides the shape, and on the write mix the shape is the finding.
Both zou legs decay inside the measurement window and vanilla does not.

| leg | 0 to 30 s | 30 to 60 s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pg18 | 1540.8 tps, p50 4.752, p99 11.054 | 1545.7 tps, p50 4.527, p99 13.849 |
| zou-localfs | 1030.1 tps, p50 6.926, p99 15.371 | 484.6 tps, p50 13.577, p99 48.722 |
| zou-minio | 928.5 tps, p50 8.000, p99 16.138 | 756.4 tps, p50 8.172, p99 22.658 |

The filesystem leg loses 53 per cent of its throughput between the first half of the run and the second, and its p99 triples.
So 763 tps is not a steady state, it is the average of a good half minute and a bad one, and 485 tps is the closer estimate of what that leg sustains, which is 31 per cent of vanilla rather than 49.
The MinIO leg decays too, by 19 per cent.
A 60 s run is long enough to see this and too short to see where it settles, which is the first thing the scale 1000 runs have to fix.

## What the store wrote

The write leg counters, differenced across the measurement window only, so the load is not in these numbers.

| | zou-localfs | zou-minio |
| --- | --- | --- |
| transactions | 45813 | 50572 |
| wal produced | 25.25 MiB | 27.57 MiB |
| store bytes added | 3.40 GiB | 5.25 GiB |
| write amplification | 138.1 | 194.9 |
| per transaction | 78 KiB | 109 KiB |
| shard rewrites | 58 objects, 3.39 GiB | 36 objects, 5.23 GiB |
| wal put_if_match | 8060, 15.31 MiB | 6841, 16.27 MiB |
| gets | 191448, 2.78 GiB | 16716, 4.78 GiB |
| ranged gets | 23167, 1.79 GiB | 224, 4.4 MiB |
| cas conflicts | 0 | 0 |

A pgbench transaction produces around 575 bytes of WAL and this store wrote 78 KiB and 109 KiB for each one.
The whole of that ratio is in one row: 58 shard rewrites carrying 3.39 GiB, an average of 60 MiB written per rewrite, and 36 rewrites carrying 5.23 GiB, an average of 149 MiB.
The WAL itself is written honestly, 8060 conditional puts for 15 MiB, about two kilobytes a put.
Everything else is the same shard object being written out whole because some of it changed, which is the layout question the storage redesign notes are about and not something a tuning pass reaches.

The read only leg says the same thing from the other side.
On the filesystem store a workload that writes nothing still added 169 MiB to the store against 2.16 MiB of WAL, an amplification of 78, all of it three shard rewrites totalling 168 MiB, finishing work the write leg left behind.
On MinIO the same leg added 1.18 MiB, an amplification of 0.5, because that store's rewrite had already happened.

## The bill

Priced from the measured op counts and bytes on the `aws-s3-standard` card dated 2026-08-29, which is what these runs would have cost had the store been a real bucket in the reader's region.

| | zou-localfs | zou-minio |
| --- | --- | --- |
| billed gets in the 60 s window | 214615 | 16940 |
| billed puts in the same window | 8135 | 6898 |
| ops cost for the window | 0.1265 usd | 0.0413 usd |
| per million transactions | 2.7619 usd | 0.8160 usd |
| storage a month | 0.1422 usd | 0.2610 usd |
| per GB ingested | 0.0372 usd | 0.0079 usd |

The two legs ran the same binary against the same workload and one of them would have cost 3.4x the other on the same price card, entirely because it issued 9.3x the requests.
That is the number that says a filesystem store is not a preview of an S3 store: a read pattern that is free on a local directory is the dominant line on the bill against a bucket, and 182064 of the filesystem leg's 191448 gets were small page class reads that cost nothing locally and 40 cents a million on the card.

Read the per million figure as a unit and not as a monthly bill, because nobody runs pgbench flat out for a month, but the unit is what a sustained tenant multiplies: 842 tps held for a month is 2.18 billion transactions, which on the MinIO leg's op profile is about 1780 usd of requests.
At an amplification of 195 that is a layout cost rather than a workload cost, and it is the strongest argument in this book for the redesign.

Three of the seven cards charge nothing for a request, so the whole finding above disappears on them.
Backblaze, Wasabi and a box in a rack bill the bytes held and nothing else, and on those the filesystem leg is the cheaper of the two by a little under two, since it left 6.2 GiB behind against the MinIO leg's 11.4, exactly reversing the ranking S3 Standard gives.
Both readings are correct and they answer different questions, which is the reason to print seven rows instead of one: the request count is what a design decision moves, and whether that matters at all is a property of the store somebody picked.

Express is the card worth looking at twice.
It charges a quarter of Standard's request price and then bills the bytes on top, and on the MinIO leg that lands at 0.0280 against Standard's 0.0413, so the store that writes fewer and larger objects wins there.
Its storage line is the opposite: 1.25 usd a month against Standard's 0.26 for the same footprint, nearly five times, and a tenant that holds data between benchmark windows pays that every month whether it runs anything or not.
The two lines point opposite ways and the only honest thing to do is print both rather than a total, which is what the table does.

The `minio-server3` row is a box amortised at 15 usd a month over 380 GiB, so its storage figure is the highest of the free request cards.
That number is a placeholder against a real invoice, as its own note says, and no dollar figure in this book should be quoted from it until the invoice is read.

## The split between the two zou legs

The two legs took visibly different routes through the same binary.
The filesystem leg served 23167 ranged reads out of shard objects and 182064 small page class gets; the MinIO leg served 224 ranged reads and 8508 page class gets, and pulled 235 whole shard objects worth 4.75 GiB instead.
Whole object fetches are compaction pulling its inputs and ranged reads are the serving path asking for blocks, so on the face of it the MinIO leg compacted more and served less from the store.

Two things could produce that and this page does not separate them.
The stores started in different states, 55 MB against 3.29 GB, so the two legs had different amounts of history to fold.
And the MinIO leg parked 91 reads waiting on the page service, with a p99 wait of 1.05 s, which is where its 294 ms p999 and 1.39 s worst transaction come from, so some of its serving traffic was waiting rather than reading.
The filesystem leg parked none.
Until that is separated, neither leg's read counters should be quoted as the read profile of the storage engine.

## What this does not answer

Real S3 is not measured, so tamnd/zou#1 line 58 stays unticked.
The two zou columns are a local directory and a MinIO on loopback, and both of them are a store with no network in front of it, which the bill section shows is exactly the assumption that hides the expensive behaviour.

Scale 1000 is not measured, so tamnd/zou#31 line 29 stays unticked with the scale 100 half of it now done.

tamnd/zou#31 line 41 wants the accounts update p99 under 25 ms at this shape and this run cannot answer it: the harness records per statement means and not per statement percentiles, so what exists is a 0.352 ms mean for that update on the filesystem leg against a 36.8 ms p99 for the whole transaction, and the p99 the line asks for is not in the file.
That is a harness gap rather than a missing run.
