# 2026-08-17: a hundred thousand sockets on one node, and what a changed row costs when everyone is waiting

The M4 line asks for a hundred thousand concurrent sockets on one node at a thousand changed rows a second with a published p99.
This is the day that was measured, three runs of it, and the answer is half a yes: the sockets are held and every delivery is accounted for, and the rows a second are not the node's to give.

Two boxes, neither of them quiet, which is said here rather than in a footnote.
server3 is the node under test, 8 cores, 23 GB, an AMD EPYC, with the store on a MinIO on its own loopback and its disk 95 percent full.
It also runs the owner's crawler at between one and two of its cores and that MinIO at about a third of one, load average two to four before a run starts.
server2 is the generator, 6 cores, 12 GB, on the other side of the internet, running a browser and other work of the owner's at about one and a half cores.
The generator has to be the other box: a hundred thousand descriptors and a goroutine each on the node under test would make the node's cpu partly the benchmark.

The node is `zou serve` with six http ports, one postgres port, the ops port, `--pool 0`, `--max-attached 4`, `--shared-buffers 256MB`, and the realtime ceilings raised rather than turned off so the counting stays in the measured path and only the refusal is out of the way.

The socket count is the first thing and it is not in doubt.
A hundred thousand of a hundred thousand accepted on `sockets-100k`, none refused, six lost to read timeouts across eleven minutes of public internet, and the node's own gauges agreeing at 99994 and 99994.
Memory is 23.4 KB a socket, which is the whole reason the run fits on the box, and the story of that number is in the zou repository's implementation note: the websocket library allocates a 128 KB read buffer per socket and zeroes it before every read, and a hundred thousand of those want more memory than the box has.

## What the node costs per change, which is the number this repository can defend

Reading a change out of the stream and writing it to every socket owed it: 47.1 ms at the mean on `sockets-100k`, where a hundred sockets are owed each row, and 29.4 ms on `sockets-100k-wide`, where nine are.
From the database's own commit timestamp to the frame leaving: 126.7 ms and 106.6 ms.
Both are the node measuring itself, over 1,882,394 and 1,365,872 changes, and both sit in the result file beside the client's view so neither has to be taken on faith.

The node was under half its cpu in every one of these runs, 22 to 43 percent of eight cores it shares with somebody else's crawler.

## What is actually in the way

The rate.
`sockets-100k` asked for a thousand rows a second and committed 62.8, and that is arithmetic rather than mystery: eight writers, twenty five rows a transaction, a commit mean of 3.57 s, and eight times twenty five over 3.57 is 62.8.
More writers is not the answer, because a project's postmaster takes forty connections in total, so even all forty at twenty five rows a transaction is 280 rows a second.
A run at sixty four writers proved that the short way, by failing to connect: `08006: sorry, too many clients already`.

Bigger transactions are the answer as far as they go.
`sockets-100k-wide` is the same node, the same binary and the same hundred thousand sockets with two hundred and fifty row transactions and sixteen writers, and it reached 455 rows a second at a 1.80 s commit mean, seven times the rate, with the node's own cost per change going down rather than up.

So the write path is the ceiling on this milestone line, at 455 of the 1000 rows a second it asks for, and it belongs to the engine rather than to the realtime tier.

## Three things about these numbers that are the harness and not the node

The client's delivery mean on `sockets-100k` is 22.8 s against a commit mean of 3.57 s plus the node's own 127 ms, and that gap is the generator: six shared cores scheduling a hundred thousand goroutines that each have to timestamp a frame.
At this socket count the node's own histograms are the measurement and the client's mean is not, which is why both are published.

The wide run's 4672 missing deliveries are the drain.
It is fifteen seconds and that run's delivery p99 is 40.8 s, so frames still in flight when the window closed are counted as never arriving.
That is the accounting being pessimistic on purpose, and 4672 of 1,366,046 is an upper bound on loss rather than a measurement of it.
The fix is a drain longer than the tail it is draining.

The node's two histograms report a p99 of exactly 500 ms at this socket count, which is a bucket edge and not a measurement.
They want finer buckets before a fan out p99 is published from them.

## The generator's own wall, which cost an afternoon

The first attempt at a hundred thousand collapsed at forty thousand sockets: the ramp fell from 350 connects a second to 55 and stayed there, while the node sat at 22 percent of its cpu with a load average of two.

`perf` on the generator: 28.95 percent in `__inet_check_established`, 25.39 in the queued spinlock, 15.70 in `__inet_hash_connect`.
Seventy percent of a six core box choosing source port numbers.
What one address has to one destination port is its ephemeral range, `32768 60999` on a stock kernel and so 28231, not the 65535 the arithmetic wants, and well before the range is full the kernel is scanning most of it per connect under a hash bucket lock.

Widening the range to `20000 65535` and serving six http ports instead of three, so each destination stays about a third full: fifty thousand sockets in 2m54s where the first run took 5m14s, sixty thousand in 3m37s where it took 8m52s, and a hundred thousand held.

This is in the README now because a ramp that collapses reads like the node's accept path and is not, and the only way to see that is to profile the client.
